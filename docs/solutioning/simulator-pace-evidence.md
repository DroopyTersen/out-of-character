# Simulator speech-pace rehearsal evidence

Four bounded, synthetic scope-scenario rehearsals used the same `good` trainee plan and the real GPT-Live 1 client with Jev director observations. The Morgan baseline ran before the pace wording changed; the Morgan and Avery follow-ups ran afterward. A second Morgan revision added an explicit conversational pace target of 220–240 words per minute. Their reports and playable audio are retained locally:

*For each non-opening client transcript entry, count words with `\b[\w'-]+\b` after normalizing curly apostrophes, divide by `(endMs - startMs) / 1000`, then take the median of those entry rates. This applies the same calculation to all four runs. It is a transcript timestamp proxy, not an acoustic articulation measurement. The after runs contain very short overlapping client fragments: Morgan's two-word “A manual” spans 0.2 s, and Avery's “I can-” and “Mm” span 0.4 and 0.2 s. These inflate individual entry rates; a single sample per character cannot establish a repeatable pace change. Morgan's median is only 0.08 words/s lower after the edit.

The changed brief digest confirms that the Morgan follow-up used different actor instructions. All three openings were acknowledged, all sessions reported confirmed finalization, and none recorded a provider or probe error, delegation, or sent private cue. All five director checks in each run selected `no_hint`. The first audible opening arrived after 2.3 s before, 2.8 s for Morgan after, and 2.2 s for Avery after.

The dialogue shows useful, character-specific bargaining. Before the edit, Morgan accepted a clean manual report subject to reliable overdue data and agreed to a product-owner decision, while insisting that a later low-effort dashboard stay in scope for discussion. After the edit, Morgan challenged whether the manual report would hold up in the executive review, asked who would build it and from which data, and ended by pressing for a concrete option that would not damage the schedule. Avery was more guarded: they paused over the manual-report substitute, raised the risk of presenting unverified figures, identified missing access to overdue history, then accepted the Thursday data check and product-owner meeting as a credible path. This supports different *wording and negotiating behavior* in the transcripts; the saved WAV files require a separate listening judgment for vocal naturalness. Avery's opening transcription begins awkwardly (“Thanks Hey, uh”) and one later sentence has a capitalization glitch, so this is not a claim of flawless delivery.

The final revision is modestly faster in this sample (3.40 versus 3.29 words/s), below its prompted target. Morgan objects to a manual report as a step back, challenges whether the consultant is protecting the release or avoiding inconvenient work, and agrees to involve the product owner while keeping cost and timing unresolved. All five director observations still selected `no_hint`; finalization was confirmed with no errors or delegations. Its transcript splits a sigh across two passages, another reason not to treat this proxy as a precise acoustic measurement. This is evidence of the intended bargaining behavior and a small pace difference, not proof that a prompt guarantees a particular speech rate.

The probe sends synthesized trainee audio directly over a WebSocket. It does not test the browser's WebRTC path, a human conversation, or production checkpoint/archive behavior.
